# Cooking and recipe measurement units worldwide

Researched 2026-10-02 for the `convert_v2` closed unit set (see
`docs/adr/0001-units-v2-closed-unit-set.md` and `CONTEXT.md`).

**Scope.** This file lists units of volume, mass and temperature that appear in cooking, recipes and
food markets. It covers length only where recipes use it (pan and tin sizes). It also lists informal
recipe measures (pinch, stick, glass, tin, Päckchen and so on), marked *conventional*. For each unit
it gives the metric value and says whether that value is **defined** (by statute, regulation or
standard, which is named) or **conventional** (custom, a manufacturer, a cookbook authority or a
nutrition agency, which is named).

**The 100-year rule.** A unit not used anywhere since about 1926 is excluded. "In use" includes
informal use in recipes or markets after the unit lost legal status. Excluded units are listed at the
end of each section so they are not researched again. Where an end date is known, the legal abolition
date and the date practical use died out are given separately. Where it is not known, the entry says
"unknown".

**Source labels.**
- `[n]` points to a numbered reference at the end of the file.
- **(2°)** means the value or date rests only on a secondary source: an encyclopedia, Wikipedia, a blog,
  news or a recipe site.
- **(snippet)** means the source was seen only as a search-engine result, not fetched.
- Any source not marked is primary: legislation, a regulator, a standards or statistics body, a
  government food or nutrition agency, an official language authority or a manufacturer's own label.
  A primary text read through an unofficial mirror is still counted as primary, and the reference
  list says so.
- "(calc.)" marks a value computed from an exact legal ratio, not printed in the source.

**Column meanings.**
- *Defined or conventional*: who fixes the value.
- *Status*: **current-legal**, **current-informal** (in use but not a legal unit for trade),
  **restricted** (legal only for named purposes) or **obsolete**. The date in brackets is when that
  status began.

## Recipe-scale cutoff (judgement, not sourced)

No source defines when a unit is too large or too small for recipes. This section records a decision
taken with the maintainer on 2026-10-02.

**The test is the unit, not the amount.** A 12 kg turkey is written "12 kg" or "26 lb", and the
existing units cover that. A unit is worth adding only if "1 of it" is a plausible ingredient amount.

**Cutoff: about 5 kg for mass and about 5 l for volume, per single unit.** This is just above the
largest units recipes use, including institutional recipes for 50–100 servings (pound, gallon,
#10 can).

| Side | Mass | Volume |
|---|---|---|
| Within the cutoff | pound, kg, jin/catty (0.5–0.6 kg), seer (0.93 kg), okka/oke (1.25–1.28 kg), viss (1.63 kg), dharni (2.39 kg) | US gallon (3.79 l), #10 can (about 3 qt), shō (1.8 l), imperial gallon (4.55 l), pathi (4.55 l) |
| Above the cutoff (trade and farm units) | stone (6.35 kg, body weight), arroba (11.5–15 kg), pud (16.38 kg), maund (37.3–40 kg), picul (60.48 kg), ardeb (150 kg of wheat) | peck (8.81 l), to/mal (about 18 l), debe (about 20 l), bushel (35.24 l), tin (40.91 l) |

**Borderline cases:**
- **kan / gwan (3.75 kg):** within the cutoff by size, but no recipe use was found. Leave them out
  unless a user asks.
- **Market containers (mudu, olonka, derica, margarine tin):** within the cutoff by size, but their
  values vary by region and commodity.

**Lower end.** Drop, minim and grain are the smallest units with any recipe use.

**Best-unit display.** The existing `kiloliter` and `hectoliter` are harmless for conversion, but the
best-unit tables should stop at kg/l and lb/gal, as `units.toml` already does.

`src/convert/units/definitions.rs` has 38 units. Mapping from the entries above:

| convert_v2 id | Covers |
|---|---|

| `kilogram`, `hectogram`, `decagram`, `gram`, `decigram`, `centigram`, `milligram` | all metric mass. Italian **etto** = `hectogram`; Dutch and Indonesian **ons** (100 g) = `hectogram`; **dag/dkg/Deka** = `decagram`; 公斤 = `kilogram`. |
| `us_teaspoon`, `us_tablespoon`, `us_fluid_ounce`, `us_cup`, `us_pint`, `us_quart`, `us_gallon` | US customary liquid measures only, at their exact HB 44 values. |
| `ounce`, `pound` | avoirdupois oz and lb (US, UK, CA). These are also the UK and Canadian legal values. |
| `inch`, `foot` | international inch and foot: US and UK pan sizes |
| `meter`, `centimeter`, `millimeter`, and the other metric lengths | metric pan sizes |
| `celsius`, `fahrenheit` | temperature points, including NIST oven bands written in °C or °F |
| `second`, `minute`, `hour`, `day` | (time; outside this research) |

Points the locale design needs to know:

- **The "imperial" volume units are US customary.** CONTEXT.md says so ("a cup is 236.59 ml, not
  284 ml"). The British and Canadian imperial pint (568.26 ml), fl oz (28.41 ml), gallon (4.546 l),
  quart and gill are **not covered**. A UK or Canadian recipe that says "1 pint" is currently converted
  as 473 ml, about 17 % short.
- **No metric spoon or cup exists.** The 15 ml tablespoon and 5 ml teaspoon used almost everywhere
  outside the US are within 1.5 % of the US units, so aliasing them to `us_tablespoon` and `us_teaspoon`
  is close. These units have **no unit of their own** in convert_v2:
  - the 250 ml metric cup
  - the 240 ml FDA cup
  - the 200 ml Japanese and Korean cup
  - the 20 ml Australian tablespoon
  - the 180.39 ml 合

  Of these, only the 240 ml cup is within 1.5 % of `us_cup`. The others are 5.7 % to 35 % away
  from any existing unit.
- **Kitchen units with exact metric values** could be written as milliliter or gram multiples:
  - mass: Pfund, pond, livre, pon and Colombian libra = 500 g; jin = 500 g; tai jin = 600 g; tola = 11.6638 g.
  - volume: krm = 1 ml.

  They still need their own unit ids, or the alias layer needs scaled aliases. The `Locale` maps a
  string to a `Unit` with no factor, so "1 Pfund" cannot currently resolve to 500 g.
- Not covered at all: US dry measures, the HK catty and tael, the gas mark and other oven scales,
  and every conventional measure (pinch, stick, Päckchen, can sizes).

## Open questions / unverified

**Not confirmed from a primary source:**
- **Australia:** the standard that defines the 20 ml tablespoon (number and year; possibly AS 2258).
  The 1992 Commonwealth report states the value [22].
- **Standards bodies:** no NZS, SABS, British Standard, JIS or Swedish SIS standard was found for
  measuring cups or spoons. The JIS numbers "S 2050/S 3009/S 2052" from a search summary could not be
  confirmed.
- **Canada:** the Canadian Government Specifications Board cooking-measure standard (number, sizes).
- **Japan:**
  - The 200 ml cup and 15/5 ml spoons trace to 女子栄養大学 (1948), not to a government body. The MEXT
    tables were checked and do not define them.
  - Cabinet Order No. 329 of 1958, which sets the land and buildings shakkanhō end date (1966), was not
    read.
- **PRC:** the 1959 State Council order (10 两 = 1 斤, 500 g) was not read. The 1984 order was [36].
- **Korea:** no primary text for the 2007-07-01 enforcement date against 근 and 돈, or for RDA's
  200 ml 컵.
- **Hong Kong:** the current consolidated Cap 68 text; only the 1987 version was read
  (elegislation.gov.hk was blocked).
- **Gas mark:** no standard defines it. The Celsius equivalents are rounded inconsistently across
  sources.
- **French thermostat and German Stufe:** secondary only.
- **US can sizes:** the volumes come from a university extension page. No Can Manufacturers Institute
  document was found.
- **Russia:** ГОСТ 8022-75 (the faceted glass) and the 18 ml столовая ложка in the original Soviet
  cookbook table.
- **Netherlands:** the abolition of ons and pond is dated 1869 by one source and 1937 by another.
  Belgian usage is not checked.
- **Unverified values:**
  - Indonesian kati (625 vs 750 g).
  - Mexican and Spanish libra (about 460 g).
  - Colombian arroba (12.5 kg) in the text of Ley 33/1905.
  - Brazilian arroba (15 kg).
  - Iranian man (3 kg) and sir (75 g).
  - Indian pav and chhatak in grams.
  - Masha and ratti.
  - The Myanmar pyi script.
  - The Cambodian neal.
- **India:** the enacted text of Act 89/1956 (only the Bill was read), and the 1960/1962 compulsory
  dates.
- **Thailand:** whether MOPH Notifications 445–448 (2024) replaced Notification 182.
- **Ireland and the UK:** none open.
- **Practical end of use unknown for:**
  - imperial gill, breakfast cup, teacup, saltspoon and wineglass in recipes
  - Turkish okka
  - Egyptian ratl and oke
  - Greek oka
  - Japanese 勺, 斗 and 貫
  - PRC 市升
  - Macau 石, 甘特 and 撮
  - Russian funt
- **Not researched:**
  - Mongolia, Laos, Brunei
  - Saudi Arabia (SASO), the Gulf, Morocco and the Maghreb, Sudan
  - Syrian and Lebanese law
  - South African metrication
  - the Caribbean and Costa Rica
  - Ethiopian kunna and Nigerian olodo
  - Turkish kahve fincanı and Iranian stil
  - Spanish and Portuguese pre-metric units (onza, quartilho, alqueire, fanega)
  - Swiss primary law (fedlex)
  - French primary law (Legifrance)

**Conflicts between sources:**
- Japan's dates. The brief assumed the Measurement Act took effect in 1959. Its transition act put it
  in force on 1952-03-01, and shakkanhō stayed legal until 1958-12-31 [30].
- **Macau's tael** is printed as 37.799 31 g, while 1/16 of its own cate is 37.799 364 g [41].
- **NIST Metric Kitchen** lists 1 gal = 3.84 l (16 × 240 ml), which is inconsistent with HB 44
  (3.785 l) [1][7].
- **NIST HB 44's British notes** still define the imperial gallon as 10 lb of water. UK law defines it
  as exactly 4.546 09 l [1][10].
- **Brazil:** labelling law has a 200 ml xícara and a 10 ml colher de sopa, while recipes use 240 ml
  and 15 ml [142][144].
- **Turkey:** the Ministry of Health says the tablespoon is 10–12 ml, not 15 ml [119].
- **Russian decree dates:** the 1918 decree text sets 1922 and 1924. Secondary sources say the
  deadline was moved to 1927 [102][103].
